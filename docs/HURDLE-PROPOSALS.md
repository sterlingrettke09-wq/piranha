### 1. No parking minimums — abolished citywide
- **category** parking · **status** info · **sizeDependent** False · **addsMonths** null (no published duration)
- **trigger** Applies to all uses citywide; not size-triggered. Carve-outs: accessible spaces still required, and a PUD with its own off-site parking requirements controls.

You still have to provide accessible (ADA) spaces, calculated from what the repealed tables used to require, and a Planned Unit Development that writes its own parking numbers overrides the citywide rule. Otherwise parking is a market decision, not a mandate — a large cost saver.

This article shall govern over a conflicting provision of this title or other ordinance, unless the conflicting provision is less restrictive. This article applies to all uses and to specific regulating plans, Transit Oriented Development areas (TODs), and Neighborhood Conservation Combining Districts (NCCDs) that incorporate this chapter by reference. ### 2. No mandatory inclusionary requirement — affordability is a voluntary bonus
- **category** affordability · **status** info · **sizeDependent** False · **addsMonths** null (no published duration)
- **trigger** None. There is no unit-count or floor-area threshold at which affordable units become compulsory anywhere in the city.
- **citation** Austin LDC § 25-1-720(A) (Affordability Unlocked Bonus Program; Ord. No. 20190509-027, Pt. 2, eff. 5-20-19), read at Supp. No. 173; and Tex. Loc. Gov't Code § 214.905 (added Acts 2005, 79th Leg., ch. 1103 (H.B. 2266), eff. Sept. 1, 2005; renumbered 2007).
- **note** Austin has no inclusionary requirement: no unit count and no floor area obliges you to include income-restricted units or pay a fee in lieu. Affordability Unlocked, S.M.A.R.T. Housing and the density-bonus programs are opt-in trades — you take on affordable units only if you want the extra entitlement. State law is the reason: a Texas city may not cap the sale price of privately built housing, and the same statute expressly preserves voluntary bonus programs.
- **verbatim** The purpose of this division is to establish a voluntary affordable housing bonus program that allows for increased density for residential dwelling units.

### 3. Site plan review is administrative — no discretionary design board
- **category** review · **status** info · **sizeDependent** False · **addsMonths** null (no published duration)
- **trigger** Land Use Commission (public hearing) review fires only on a conditional use, development in a Hill Country Roadway Corridor, or where another code section requires it. No floor-area or unit-count trigger exists.
- **citation** Austin LDC § 25-5-142 (Ord. 990225-70; Ord. 010607-8; Ord. 031211-11; Ord. 20130228-074) and § 25-5-112(A); site plan requirement at § 25-5-1. Read at Supp. No. 173.
- **note** Austin has no large-project design review. Chapter 25-5 sets out exactly when a site plan goes to the Land Use Commission, and size is not on the list — only a conditional use, the Hill Country Roadway Corridor, or another code provision that names one. Everything else is decided by the director against the written code, and the director must approve a compliant plan. There is no Austin analogue to Boston's Article 80 or San Francisco's Discretionary Review.
- **verbatim** Land Use Commission approval of site plan is required for: (1) a conditional use; (2) except as provided in Section 25-5-2 (Site Plan Exemptions), development in a Hill Country Roadway Corridor; and (3) if otherwise required by this title.

### 4. Street impact fee
- **category** fees · **status** required · **sizeDependent** False · **addsMonths** null (no published duration)
- **trigger** All new development inside the city limits; assessed at final plat approval (or at building-permit application where platting is exempt) and collected at building permit issuance. No size threshold — the amount scales with service units.
- **citation** Austin LDC §§ 25-6-657, 25-6-662, 25-6-663(B) (Ord. No. 20201210-062, Pt. 2, eff. 12-21-20); authority Tex. Loc. Gov't Code ch. 395. Read at Supp. No. 173.
- **note** Austin charges a street impact fee on new development citywide, calculated from the trips your land use generates and paid when the building permit issues. The dollars per service unit are set by a separate fee ordinance, so budget from the current adopted schedule — the code itself states no amount. Reductions are available for transit proximity, parking management and affordability, but the burden of qualifying is on you.
- **verbatim** A street impact fee shall be collected at the time of the issuance of a building permit.

### 5. Water and wastewater capital recovery (impact) fees
- **category** fees · **status** required · **sizeDependent** False · **addsMonths** null (no published duration)

A tap permit will not be released until they are paid. Amounts are set by a separate ordinance, not by the code text.
- **verbatim** Except as provided by this section, this article applies to new development in the water and wastewater impact fee service area.

### 6. Parkland dedication or fee in lieu
- **category** fees · **status** required · **sizeDependent** False · **addsMonths** null (no published duration)
- **trigger** Any subdivision or site plan that includes residential units or a hotel-motel use. No minimum unit count — the obligation scales per unit from the first one.
- **citation** Austin LDC §§ 25-1-601(C)-(D), 25-1-603(C)-(D), 25-1-608(B)-(E) (Ord. No. 20231130-087, Pt. 2, eff. 1-1-24). Read at Supp. No. 173.
- **note** Every residential project in Austin must provide for parkland before its development application is approved — either by dedicating land, paying a parkland dedication fee, or both, with the director choosing. For multifamily the arithmetic is 0.005 acre per unit (0.004 per hotel/motel room), capped at 10% of the gross site area; the fee version multiplies that same figure by the average land value for your geographic area and divides by a density factor of 1 suburban, 4 urban, 40 central business district. Income-restricted and S.M.A.R.T. Housing units are exempt.
- **verbatim** [# of multifamily units × .005] + [# of hotel/motel rooms × .004] = Acres of parkland

### 7. Historic review of any building 50 years or older
- **category** demolition · **status** likely · **sizeDependent** False · **addsMonths** 4
- **trigger** A demolition, relocation, or building permit for a structure 50 years or older, in a National Register district, on a survey of historic structures, or flagged by the historic preservation officer as having designation potential.
- **citation** Austin LDC § 25-11-213(B), (C), (F), (G) (Ord. No. 20221115-049, Pt. 8, eff. 11-28-22); pendency of designation § 25-11-214. Read at Supp. No. 173.
- **note** Austin routes demolition, relocation and building permits for older structures to the Historic Landmark Commission even when nothing on the site is designated. A structure escapes this only if the historic preservation officer finds all three tests met — under 50 years old, failing at least two landmark criteria, and not contributing to a historic area district — so an ordinary 1960s house is inside the net by default. Published outer bounds: a hearing within 60 days of a complete application and a permit hold of up to 75 days after the first Commission meeting, roughly four months worst case; 180 days for a contributing structure in a National Register district. The permit can issue sooner if the Commission declines to initiate a designation case, but if it initiates one, designation becomes pending and any permit issued is void.
- **verbatim** This section does not apply to a structure if the historic preservation officer determines that the structure: (1) is less than 50 years old; (2) does not meet at least two of the criteria for designation as a historic landmark (H) combining district prescribed by Section 25-2-352(A)(3)(b) (Historic Designation Criteria); and (3) is not a contributing structure in a historic area (HD) combining district.

### 8. 120-day tenant notice before applying to demolish multifamily

Note the threshold counts the units already on the site, not the units you propose.
- **verbatim** for a multi-family property, at least 120 days prior to the date application for a building permit or a demolition permit was submitted; or ... for a mobile home park, at least 270 days prior to the date the application for a rezone, site plan, or change of use permit was submitted.

### 9. Save Our Springs: impervious cover cap in the Barton Springs watersheds
- **category** environmental · **status** likely · **sizeDependent** False · **addsMonths** null (no published duration)
- **trigger** Any development in the watersheds contributing to Barton Springs. Geographic, not size-triggered. No variances, waivers or special exceptions are available.

### 11. Valid petition: 20% of neighbours can force a 3/4 Council vote
- **category** review · **status** info · **sizeDependent** False · **addsMonths** null (no published duration)
- **trigger** A rezoning protested in writing by owners of at least 20% of the land in the proposed change or within 200 feet of it; also a PUD rezoning recommended for denial by the Land Use Commission. Discretionary path only — an as-of-right project never sees it.
- **citation** Austin LDC § 25-2-284(A) (Ord. 990225-70; Ord. 010329-18; Ord. 031211-11; Ord. No. 20160211-008, Pt. 1, eff. 2-22-16); authority Tex. Loc. Gov't Code ch. 211. Read at Supp. No. 173.
- **note** This only bites if you need a rezoning. Owners of 20% of the land in or within 200 feet of your rezoning area can file a written protest, and that single filing raises the bar from a simple majority to three-quarters of Council — 9 of 11 votes. It is the cheapest and most effective tool an opposing neighbourhood has in Austin, it costs the objectors nothing, and it is why the discretionary path here carries far more risk than the as-of-right path. A PUD that the Land Use Commission recommends denying needs the same supermajority.
- **verbatim** The affirmative vote of three-fourths of the members of council is required to approve: ... (3) a proposed rezoning that is protested in writing by the owners of not less than 20 percent of the area of land: (a) included in the proposed change; or (b) immediately adjoining the area included in the proposed rezoning and extending 200 feet from the area.

### 12. Certificate of Appropriateness (Historic Landmark Commission)
- **category** historic · **status** required · **sizeDependent** False · **addsMonths** null (no published duration)
- **trigger** Exterior alteration, removal or demolition of a designated historic landmark or contributing structure, or construction of any new standalone structure on an (H) landmark property or within an (HD) combining district. Also applies while a designation is pending.
- **citation** Austin LDC § 25-11-212(A)-(B) (Ord. 041202-16; Ord. 20090806-068; Ord. No. 20221115-049, Pt. 7, eff. 11-28-22); review standards § 25-11-213(K). Read at Supp. No. 173.
- **note** On a designated historic landmark property or inside a historic area (HD) combining district, you cannot alter, demolish or build new until the Historic Landmark Commission (or the building official) issues a Certificate of Appropriateness — and that applies whether or not a building permit is otherwise required. Small work can be signed off administratively: a one-storey ground-floor addition or outbuilding under 600 sq ft, a rear second-storey addition not visible from the street, or a pool, deck or fence. Ground-up new construction is not on that list.
- **verbatim** Until a person obtains a certificate of appropriateness from the Commission or the building official, the person may not: ... (3) construct a new, standalone, ground-up structure on a historic landmark (H) property or within a historic area (HD) combining district.

### 1. Inclusionary Zoning (IZ): set-aside required at 10+ units
- **category** affordability · **status** required · **sizeDependent** True · **addsMonths** null (no published duration)
- **trigger** Residential development proposing 10 or more new dwelling units (including cellar and penthouse units), in a zone where Subtitle C Chapter 10 is identified as applicable. Also fires on any residential building with penthouse habitable space under 11-C DCMR 1500.11, and on 10+ units built in phases on contiguous or alley-divided lots under common ownership within the prior year.
- **citation** 11-C DCMR (Zoning Regulations of 2016) § 1001.2(a)(1); set-aside at §§ 1003.1–1003.3. Chapter last amended by ZC Final Rulemaking & Order No. 04-33I, published at 66 DCR 13705 (October 18, 2019).
- **note** A residential project adding 10 or more new dwelling units in a zone where Chapter 10 is identified as applicable must set aside floor area for income-restricted Inclusionary Units. The set-aside is 10% of residential gross floor area (excluding penthouse habitable space) where the project is NOT Type I construction AND sits in a zone whose by-right height limit is 50 ft or less; it is 8% where the project is Type I construction OR the by-right height limit exceeds 50 ft (11-C DCMR 1003.1, 1003.2). Rental Inclusionary Units go to households at or below 60% of MFI, ownership units at or below 80% of MFI (1003.3). Unit counts across building permits combine over a 3-year window (1001.3). There is no in-lieu fee option in the base rule — the units must be built.
- **verbatim** Is proposing new gross floor area beyond that existing at the time of the building permit application that would result in ten (10) or more new dwelling units, including dwelling units located in a cellar or penthouse;

### 2. Large Tract Review (Office of Planning)
- **category** review · **status** required · **sizeDependent** True · **addsMonths** 2
- **trigger** A site of three (3) acres or more; OR a commercial or mixed-use commercial development of 50,000 sq ft or more of gross floor area (above grade) plus cellar area. A purely residential project below three acres does not trigger it.
- **citation** 10-B DCMR § 2300.1(a) (thresholds), § 2303.1 (60-day schedule), § 2304.1 (exemptions). Final Rulemaking published at 33 DCR 7701 (December 12, 1986).
- **note** Before a building permit may be filed, the Office of Planning runs a 60-day interagency and community review of the project. Note what this is NOT: it produces a technical report, not an approval or denial, so it is a schedule item rather than a veto point — DC has no as-of-right entitlement gate comparable to Boston's Article 80. Notice goes to the affected ANC, any known civic association, and every property owner within 200 feet, and there is at least one community meeting. Exempt sites under § 2304.1 include projects filed as Planned Unit Developments and areas in the former C-3-C, C-4 and C-5 downtown districts (now the D zones).
- **verbatim** Review, prior to the filing of applications for building or construction permits, all large tract development projects comprising three (3) acres or more and any commercial or mixed-use commercial development of fifty thousand square feet (50,000 ft.2) or more gross floor area (above grade) and cellar area (below grade)

### 3. Historic review: HPRB, and the Commission of Fine Arts in Georgetown / near federal land
- **category** historic · **status** required · **sizeDependent** False · **addsMonths** 4
- **trigger** Any permit to construct a building or structure in a historic district or on the site of a historic landmark. Not size-triggered.
- **citation** D.C. Official Code § 6-1107(b), (c), (f) (new construction); § 6-1105 (alterations). Historic Landmark and Historic District Protection Act of 1978, D.C. Law 2-144 (Mar. 3, 1979), as amended by D.C. Law 20-249 (Apr. 30, 2015).
- **note** New construction in a DC historic district or on a landmark site goes to the Historic Preservation Review Board — unless the site is subject to the Commission of Fine Arts under the Old Georgetown Act (Georgetown) or the Shipstead-Luce Act (sites near the Mall, the Capitol grounds, Rock Creek Park and other federal property), in which case a federal commission reviews the design instead. The statute gives the Mayor 120 days after the Review Board receives the referral to make the finding. The DC standard is compatibility, not appropriateness: a permit issues unless the design and the district's character are found incompatible.
- **verbatim** Prior to making the finding on the permit application required by subsection (f) of this section, the Mayor may refer the application to the Historic Preservation Review Board for a recommendation, but shall so refer all applications that are not subject to review by the Commission of Fine Arts under the Old Georgetown Act (§ 6-1201 et seq.) or the Shipstead-Luce Act (§ 6-611.01).

### 4. Demolition in a historic district: "necessary in the public interest"
- **category** demolition · **status** required · **sizeDependent** False · **addsMonths** 4
- **trigger** Any permit to demolish a historic landmark or a building or structure located in a historic district. Not size-triggered.
- **citation** D.C. Official Code § 6-1104(b), (c), (e) (demolitions). D.C. Law 2-144 (Mar. 3, 1979), as amended by D.C. Law 20-249 (Apr. 30, 2015).
- **note** This is a materially harder gate than the design review that accompanies it, and it is easy to miss. To tear down a landmark or any building in a historic district, the applicant must show the demolition is necessary in the public interest, or that refusing the permit would cause unreasonable economic hardship — a burden the applicant carries, with a public hearing and a 120-day clock. Where demolition is allowed only because the replacement is a "project of special merit," the demolition permit cannot issue until the new-construction permit issues simultaneously, so the teardown cannot precede a fully approved replacement design.
- **verbatim** No permit shall be issued unless the Mayor finds that issuance of the permit is necessary in the public interest, or that failure to issue a permit will result in unreasonable economic hardship to the owner.

### 5. DC Environmental Policy Act: Environmental Impact Screening Form
- **category** environmental · **status** likely · **sizeDependent** False · **addsMonths** null (no published duration)
- **trigger** Any action requiring a District permit that costs over $1,000,000 in 1989 dollars (CPI-adjusted) and may have a significant impact on the environment. Exempt: projects in the Central Employment Area (§ 8-109.06(a)(7)) and residential projects in the former R-1 through R-5-A zones (20 DCMR § 7202.2).
- **citation** D.C. Official Code §§ 8-109.02(2), 8-109.03, 8-109.06(a)(7) (District of Columbia Environmental Policy Act of 1989, D.C. Law 8-36, Oct. 18, 1989); implementing rules 20 DCMR §§ 7201.1, 7202.1–7202.2, Final Rulemaking published at 44 DCR 2799 (May 9, 1997).
- **note** DC has its own environmental review statute on top of the federal baseline, and it reaches private projects because issuing a building permit is itself an "action" under the Act. The screening form is triggered by project COST, not floor area: over $1,000,000 in 1989 dollars, CPI-adjusted (roughly $2.6M today), where the project may significantly affect the environment. If screening indicates significant impact, a full Environmental Impact Statement follows, and no agency may issue the permit until review completes. Two exemptions do most of the work in DC: projects within the Central Employment Area are exempt by statute, and 20 DCMR § 7202.2 exempts residential projects in the former R-1 through R-5-A zones. Confirm both against the current zone map — those zone names predate the 2016 rewrite.
- **verbatim** An Environmental Impact Screening Form (EISF) shall be prepared for any action that would cost over one million dollars ($1,000,000) based on 1989 dollars adjusted annually according to the Consumer Price Index and that may have a significant impact on the environment.

### 6. Stormwater Retention Volume (5,000 sq ft of land disturbance)
- **category** environmental · **status** required · **sizeDependent** False · **addsMonths** null (no published duration)
- **trigger** Disturbance of 5,000 sq ft or more of land area (or a common plan of development that does), or a major substantial improvement activity whose combined improved-building and land-disturbance footprint is 5,000 sq ft or greater.
- **citation** 21 DCMR § 599 (definition of "Major land-disturbing activity"), §§ 520.3 and 520.4(a). Final Rulemakings published at 60 DCR 10640 and 60 DCR 10732 (July 19, 2013).
- **note** Any project disturbing 5,000 sq ft or more of land must retain on site the runoff from a 1.2-inch rainfall event, with at least half of that volume retained on the site itself absent DOEE relief for extraordinarily difficult conditions. On a tight urban lot this drives real design cost — green roofs, cisterns, permeable paving — and the shortfall can be met by buying Stormwater Retention Credits, which is a live and priced DC market. The threshold is land disturbance, not building size, so a small building on a large site still triggers it.
- **verbatim** Activity that disturbs, or is part of a common plan of development that disturbs, five thousand square feet (5,000 ft2) or greater of land area, except that multiple distinct areas that each disturb less than 5,000 ft2 of land and that are in separate, non-adjacent sites do not constitute a major land-disturbing activity.

### 7. Green Area Ratio: landscape minimum on every new building
- **category** environmental · **status** required · **sizeDependent** False · **addsMonths** null (no published duration)
- **trigger** All new buildings on properties in all zones except the R and RF zones. Also fires on existing buildings where additions or interior renovations within any 12-month period exceed 100% of the building's assessed value. Not size-triggered.

- **note** DC requires a minimum score for on-site landscape and stormwater elements — trees, vegetated roofs, permeable paving, bioretention — on every new building outside the R and RF house-form zones. A landscape plan prepared by a Certified Landscape Expert is a permit submission requirement, and no certificate of occupancy issues until a landscape checklist is accepted. Historic resources are exempt unless an addition increases the resource's gross floor area by 50% or more.
- **verbatim** The GAR standards set forth in this chapter shall apply to all new buildings and to all existing buildings where any additions, interior renovations, or both within any twelve (12) month period exceed one hundred percent (100%) of the assessed value of the building as set forth in the records of the Office of Tax and Revenue as of the date of the building permit application

### 9. Green Building Act: LEED certification for the nonresidential portion (50,000+ sq ft)
- **category** environmental · **status** required · **sizeDependent** True · **addsMonths** null (no published duration)

- **note** Read the scope carefully — this one is often misquoted as applying to all large buildings. ### 11. TOPA: tenants get first right to buy before sale or demolition
- **category** demolition · **status** likely · **sizeDependent** False · **addsMonths** null (no published duration)

- **citation** D.C. Official Code § 42-3404.02(a); timetable for 5+ units at § 42-3404.11; new-construction exclusion at § 42-3404.02b(b)(20). Rental Housing Conversion and Sale Act of 1980, D.C. Law 3-86; amended by the RENTAL Act, D.C. Law 26-80 (Dec. 31, 2025).
- **note** This is the single most distinctive thing about acquiring a DC redevelopment site, and it fires on demolition, not just sale. Before an owner may sell an occupied rental building OR issue a notice to vacate to demolish it, the tenants must be given a bona fide opportunity to purchase. For accommodations of 5 or more units the statute sets minimum periods the owner must allow: 45 days for the tenants to register an organization, then not less than 120 days to negotiate a contract, then not less than 120 days after contracting to arrange financing — and tenants routinely assign those rights to a developer for a payment. No months are assigned here because the clock only runs if tenants organize, but a TOPA-encumbered site should never be underwritten on an as-of-right timeline. Since the RENTAL Act of 2025, a multifamily building whose permanent certificate of occupancy issued within the prior 15 years is excluded.
- **verbatim** Before an owner of a housing accommodation may sell the housing accommodation or issue a notice to vacate for purposes of demolition or discontinuance of housing use, the owner shall give the tenant an opportunity to purchase the housing accommodation at a price and terms that represent a bona fide offer of sale.

### 6. State environmental review (EAW) — threshold is high
- **category** environmental · **status** info · **sizeDependent** True · **addsMonths** null (no published duration)
- **trigger** A mandatory Environmental Assessment Worksheet is required at 375 attached units (250 unattached) where the project is consistent with the city's adopted comprehensive plan, and at 150 attached units (100 unattached) where it is not. ### 8. Every demolition is screened for historic significance
- **category** demolition · **status** required · **sizeDependent** False · **addsMonths** null (no published duration)
- **trigger** Any demolition of a principal building or structure, anywhere in the city, regardless of the building's age or whether it is designated. The planning director reviews every wrecking/demolition permit application before it can issue.
- **citation** Minneapolis Code of Ordinances §§ 599.910 and 599.920 (Title 23, ch. 599, art. IX; Chapter 599 amended in its entirety by Ord. No. 2025-053, § 1, adopted November 20, 2025)
- **note** This screen catches undesignated buildings, which is what makes it unusual. If the planning director finds the building is not a potential historic resource, the permit issues — no hearing. If it IS flagged, you need an approved "application for demolition of potential historic resource," decided by the Heritage Preservation Commission at a public hearing (§ 599.920), and the HPC can approve only on one of three findings: a certified engineer's report of a structurally unsafe condition, a finding that the property lacks significance and integrity, or a cost estimate showing rehabilitation to the Secretary of the Interior's Standards is not economically viable. - **verbatim** § 599.910(a): "All demolitions of principal buildings or structures shall be reviewed pursuant to this chapter prior to a wrecking permit being granted." — § 599.910(b): "The planning director shall review all submitted building, wrecking or demolition permit applications which constitute the demolition of a principal building or structure to determine whether the subject property has been designated as a landmark or part of a historic district, identified as a potential historic resource or may constitute a potential historic resource."

### 10. No prevailing-wage rule for privately financed projects
- **category** labor · **status** info · **sizeDependent** False · **addsMonths** null (no published duration)
- **trigger** Minneapolis's living-wage ordinance reaches only city contracts for services valued at $100,000 or more and city business subsidies valued at $100,000 or more. A privately financed development is outside it entirely.
- **citation** Minneapolis Code of Ordinances §§ 38.30 (definitions of "City contract" and "City business subsidy") and 38.40 (Title 2, ch. 38, Living Wage and Responsible Public Spending Regulations)
- **note** An absence established structurally: Chapter 38's applicability is defined entirely in terms of city contracts and city subsidies — the chapter contains no provision extending wage standards to privately financed construction. - **verbatim** § 38.30: "City contract is defined as a contract for services with the city valued at one hundred thousand dollars ($100,000.00) or more; or a subcontract valued at one hundred thousand dollars ($100,000.00) or more for providing part or all of the services covered by another entity's contract with the city valued at one hundred thousand dollars ($100,000.00) or more." and "City business subsidy is defined as a grant, contribution of personal property, real property, the principal amount of a loan at rates below those commercially available to the recipient, any reduction or deferral of any tax or any fee, any guarantee of any payment under any loan, lease, or other obligation, or any preferential use of government facilities given to a business valued at one hundred thousand dollars ($100,000.00) or more."

- **trigger** New development subject to Chapter 17.28; density factor depends on use (22 units/acre generally; 14 units/acre for single/two-family subdivisions).
- **citation** Metro Nashville & Davidson County Code § 17.28.065.A.3 and § 17.28.065.B.2 (Amdt. 1 to Ord. BL2024-687 § 2, 2025); permit requirement at § 17.40.440 (Ord. BL2022-1122 § 13, 2022). Municode Supp. No. 53, codified through Ord. No. BL2025-1141, approved December 17, 2025.
- **note** Nashville enforces a tree canopy budget on the finished site, not just a protection rule. Non-single/two-family properties must reach 22 tree density units per acre using retained or replacement trees; single/two-family subdivisions must reach 14 (excluding building lots). Separately, no retained, protected (6 inches DBH or more), or heritage tree may be removed without a permit from the zoning administrator, and trees 24 inches DBH or larger must be survey-located on the final site plan. Replacement planting to hit the density factor is a real line item on a wooded parcel. Construction of a single- or two-family dwelling on a lot already platted when the chapter was enacted is exempt (§ 17.28.020.F.4).
- **verbatim** Each property, other than those residential properties provided for below. of this section, shall attain a tree density factor of at least twenty-two units per acre using retained or replacement trees, or a combination of both. ... Unless exempt from the provisions of this chapter, no person shall remove or in any way damage any retained, protected or heritage tree without first obtaining a permit from the zoning administrator.

### 14. Preserved floodplain — encroachment needs a Stormwater Management Commission variance
- **category** flood · **status** likely · **sizeDependent** False · **addsMonths** null (no published duration)
- **trigger** Parcel is encumbered by natural floodplain or floodway (per the Nashville stormwater management manual / Chapter 15.64), regardless of project size.
- **citation** Metro Nashville & Davidson County Code § 17.28.040.A and § 17.28.040.C.1 (Ord. BL2023-1888 § 25, 2023); cross-referencing Metro Code Ch. 15.64. Municode Supp. No. 53, codified through Ord. No. BL2025-1141, approved December 17, 2025.
- **note** Nashville goes beyond the federal NFIP baseline: floodplain area designated as natural floodplain on a parcel is meant to stay preserved, and building into it requires a variance from the Stormwater Management Commission that can cover no more than twenty percent of the preserved area — and only on a finding that the encroachment reduces flood danger or improves the floodplain's environmental quality. Manipulated floodplain land also cannot count toward the base district's minimum lot size, and any lot containing natural floodplain becomes a 'critical lot' with finished-floor elevations fixed on the final plat. On a partially flood-prone parcel, treat the preserved floodplain as effectively unbuildable when sizing the envelope.
- **verbatim** Limited encroachments into the preserved floodplain may be authorized as a variance by the stormwater management commission, as set forth in Section 15.64 of the Metropolitan Code of Laws. Such variances shall be approved only if the committee finds that the encroachment reduces the flood danger or would improve and enhance the environmental quality of the affected floodplain section. Variances shall not be approved for greater than twenty percent of the floodplain area required to be preserved.
